## 3.1 Classical Market Making and Inventory Risk

The modern theoretical foundation for high-frequency market making is closely linked to inventory-aware stochastic control. A market maker earns spread income by continuously posting bid and ask quotes, but this activity exposes the agent to inventory risk: if buy and sell executions are imbalanced, the agent accumulates a position whose value fluctuates with the mid-price. The central control problem is therefore to trade off spread capture, adverse price movements, execution probability, and inventory control.

Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008) provide the canonical analytical framework for this trade-off. Their model derives a reservation price that shifts with inventory and a spread component that depends on risk aversion, volatility, horizon, and order-arrival intensity. In this thesis, the Avellaneda-Stoikov framework is important for two reasons. First, it gives a principled baseline against which learned policies can be compared. Second, it clarifies why volatility and inventory are natural state variables in a market-making problem: volatility affects the risk of carrying inventory, while inventory affects the desired direction and aggressiveness of quoting.

GuÃ©ant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia (2013) extend this line of work by developing tractable solutions for inventory-risk management in market making. Their contribution strengthens the stochastic-control interpretation of quote placement and provides a bridge between the original Avellaneda-Stoikov formulation and more implementable inventory-aware quoting rules. For the present thesis, this work supports the use of an analytical benchmark and reinforces the role of inventory aversion. The reward design used here, which penalizes squared inventory through an inventory penalty parameter, follows the same economic logic: market-making performance should not be evaluated only by gross spread capture, but also by the risk created by accumulated positions.

Fodra and Labadie (2012) further broaden the classical market-making view by considering inventory constraints and directional bets. Although their work is a preprint and should be cited with that status made explicit, it is useful for motivating the idea that market makers may condition quotes on more than inventory alone. Fodra and Labadie (2013), also a preprint, extend market making to multi-dimensional Markov processes, including state variables such as spread, stochastic volatility, and order intensities. This is especially relevant because it frames market making as a state-dependent control problem rather than a fixed-parameter optimization problem.

The thesis adopts a deliberately controlled version of this tradition. The simulator uses a Poisson-arrival fill model in which execution intensity decays with quote distance, echoing the exponential-intensity structure common in analytical market-making models. The Avellaneda-Stoikov strategy is retained as an interpretable classical baseline. At the same time, the thesis departs from the closed-form setting by training PPO agents in a Gymnasium environment and by exposing them to inventory, remaining horizon, a continuous realized-volatility proxy `sigma_hat`, and optional categorical regime labels. The classical literature therefore motivates the state and reward design, but it does not answer the thesis's central incremental-value question: whether a categorical volatility-regime label adds robust performance value once `sigma_hat` is already observed.

## 3.2 Reinforcement Learning for Market Making

Reinforcement learning provides a natural extension of classical market-making models because quote placement is sequential, stochastic, and reward-driven. Rather than deriving a closed-form quote rule under restrictive assumptions, an RL agent can learn a policy from simulated market interaction. The agent observes a state, chooses bid and ask quote parameters, receives executions and price movements, and updates its policy according to a reward signal. This setup is well aligned with high-frequency market making, where inventory, execution risk, and changing market conditions interact over many short time steps.

Spooner et al. (2018) are a central reference for reinforcement learning applied to market making. Their study demonstrates that an RL agent can learn market-making behavior in a limit-order-book simulation and that reward specification is crucial for the learned strategy. This is directly relevant to the thesis, which uses PPO to learn quoting decisions and evaluates policies through risk-adjusted metrics and inventory behavior. The reward formulation follows this logic by combining mark-to-market performance with an explicit inventory penalty, so that learned policies are not rewarded for accumulating excessive directional exposure. However, the focus here is narrower. The thesis uses PPO as a controlled testbed for a specific state-representation question.

Spooner and Savani (2020) extend the RL market-making discussion toward robustness by introducing adversarial reinforcement learning. Their work is useful because market-making policies can be sensitive to market conditions, simulator assumptions, and reward design. In this thesis, robustness is considered through out-of-sample evaluation, multiple random seeds, detector comparisons, oracle-label variants, model-misspecification checks, and signal-informativeness sweeps. The thesis's robustness question, however, is not adversarial training. It is whether the observed conclusion about regime labels remains stable when the regime signal is estimated, perfect, degraded, or compared with a continuous volatility proxy.

GaÅ¡perov and KostanjÄar (2021) are especially relevant because they study market making with signals through deep reinforcement learning. Their work supports the broader idea that external or derived signals can be incorporated into the state representation of a market-making agent. This is close to the conceptual motivation of the thesis: volatility-regime labels are a form of state information that might help an agent adapt quotes across changing conditions. The distinction is that the thesis asks whether a categorical volatility-regime signal adds incremental value when a related continuous signal, `sigma_hat`, is already included in the observation.

GaÅ¡perov et al. (2021) provide a broader review and discussion of reinforcement-learning approaches to optimal market making. Their work helps situate design choices such as the state vector, action representation, reward function, evaluation protocol, and comparison to baselines. The thesis fits within this family of RL market-making studies, but it contributes a focused ablation design: variants that observe `sigma_hat`, categorical regime labels, both, or oracle regime labels are compared under the same synthetic market-generation process.

GaÅ¡perov and KostanjÄar (2022) introduce a Hawkes-process-based limit-order-book simulator for deep reinforcement learning in market making. This study is important because it illustrates how richer event-dynamics models can make the simulated environment more detailed. By contrast, the present thesis intentionally uses a simpler Poisson-arrival fill model and a synthetic Markov volatility-regime process. This simplification allows the thesis to isolate the incremental informational role of the regime label under controlled conditions.

Taken together, the RL and DRL market-making literature motivates learned quoting policies, careful reward design, signal-rich observations, and robustness checks. The remaining gap is not whether RL can be used for market making, nor whether market states can matter. The gap is whether a particular form of state representation, an explicit categorical volatility-regime label, provides additional performance value beyond an already observed continuous volatility proxy.

## 3.3 Volatility Regimes, Non-Stationarity, and State Representation

Volatility regimes and non-stationarity are central to market-making problems. When volatility increases, inventory risk rises, execution probabilities may change, and quote placement should often become more conservative. When volatility decreases, narrower spreads may become more attractive. A market-making policy that ignores changes in market conditions may therefore be poorly calibrated to risk.

The literature on state-dependent market making provides a useful way to frame this issue. Fodra and Labadie (2013) model market making under multi-dimensional Markov processes, allowing relevant market variables to enter the control problem explicitly. This supports the idea that state representation is not a cosmetic modeling choice: the variables made available to the policy determine what adaptations are possible. In this thesis, volatility is represented in two related ways. The first is continuous, through `sigma_hat`, a rolling realized-volatility proxy available directly to the policy. The second is categorical, through low, medium, and high labels that discretize volatility-regime information.

Zimmer and Costa (2025), a recent preprint, also connect reinforcement-learning market making with non-stationary limit-order-book dynamics. Their work is useful as recent context because it emphasizes that market-making agents may need to adapt to changing order-book regimes rather than operate under one stationary environment. The thesis shares the broad concern with non-stationarity, but it does not pursue a broad non-stationary LOB model. Instead, it creates a controlled synthetic environment in which volatility regimes are generated by a Markov chain and detected through realized-volatility methods. This design makes the source of non-stationarity explicit and testable.

The key point is that the importance of regimes does not automatically imply that categorical regime labels improve a learned policy. If the continuous volatility proxy already contains the relevant information, then a label derived from related volatility information may be redundant. Conversely, if the label summarizes information not captured by `sigma_hat`, it could improve performance. The question is how market conditions should be represented in the policy state and whether the categorical representation adds incremental value in the tested synthetic environment.

## 3.4 Signal Redundancy and the Thesis Gap

Existing market-making studies often enrich the agent's state with signals, market states, or non-stationary features. This is a natural modeling direction: more informative observations can, in principle, allow a policy to adapt better. However, adding a signal does not by itself imply improved performance. If the signal is noisy, delayed, coarsened, highly correlated with another state variable, or difficult for the policy to exploit, it may provide little practical benefit.

This creates the exact thesis gap. The literature motivates volatility, regimes, and signals, but it usually does not isolate whether a categorical volatility-regime label adds incremental value once a continuous realized-volatility proxy is already observed. A categorical label may appear informative when considered alone, but its incremental contribution depends on what the policy already knows. If `sigma_hat` already tracks the volatility state sufficiently for quote adaptation, then a low/medium/high label may not improve out-of-sample performance.

The thesis is designed around this incremental-value question. It does not reject the economic relevance of volatility regimes, and it does not identify the internal computation learned by PPO. Instead, it tests a narrower proposition: in a controlled synthetic high-frequency market-making environment, do explicit categorical volatility-regime labels add robust performance value beyond `sigma_hat`?

The empirical conclusion should be stated carefully. In the tested synthetic environment, the evidence is consistent with signal redundancy: explicit labels do not provide robust additional performance beyond the continuous volatility signal. This wording matters. "Consistent with signal redundancy" is an interpretation supported by the ablations and diagnostics; it does not establish the internal PPO decision mechanism. The thesis's contribution is therefore a bounded empirical finding: in this controlled design, categorical regime labels should be evaluated for incremental value rather than assumed to improve performance.

## 3.5 Equivalence Testing and Null-Result Interpretation

A final methodological issue is how to interpret the absence of an observed improvement. In many empirical studies, a result with `p > 0.05` is treated informally as "no effect." This is not sufficient for the present thesis. Failure to reject a null hypothesis does not show that two strategies are practically similar; it may simply reflect limited power, noisy estimates, or an imprecise experimental design.

Lakens (2017) provides a practical introduction to equivalence testing and explains why researchers should distinguish between "not statistically significant" and "statistically equivalent within a meaningful range." Lakens, Scheel and Isager (2018) further develop this logic through a tutorial treatment of equivalence testing and the two one-sided tests procedure. These methodological sources are directly relevant to the thesis's interpretation of null results.

The thesis uses this logic to avoid relying only on conventional significance tests. When comparing PPO variants, the relevant question is not only whether one variant significantly outperforms another. It is also whether the observed difference is small enough to be treated as practically negligible under a pre-specified equivalence bound. TOST supports this interpretation by testing whether the performance difference lies inside a predefined interval of practical equivalence.

This framing is especially important for the regime-label result. If a regime-aware variant does not significantly outperform `sigma_only`, that alone would be weak evidence. But if the difference is also compatible with practical equivalence under a stated margin, the conclusion becomes stronger and more precise: the thesis finds no robust incremental value from explicit regime labels in the tested synthetic environment. This remains a bounded claim. It means that, under the thesis design and evaluation protocol, the categorical regime channel does not deliver reliable additional performance once `sigma_hat` is already observed.
